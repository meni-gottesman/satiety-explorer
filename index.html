About & how to read this

New here? How to read this ranking

Foods are ranked by satiety per calorie – how full they leave you for each calorie eaten. The bar and score (0–100) place each food against the others: 100 = the most filling per calorie, 0 = the least.

  • Top of the list – potatoes, pulses (beans, peas, lentils) and watery, low-energy-density vegetables: a lot of fullness for very few calories. Potato sits at #1 partly by hand: Holt measured boiled potato far above what any nutrition formula predicts, so it carries a 2.43× correction taken from that measurement. Without it potato would rank mid-table.
  • Bottom of the list – nuts, seeds and oily / fatty foods: calorie-dense, so little fullness per calorie.

Colour is a tier from very high (green) to very low (red). Search a food or pick a category to filter; tap the tier chips to show or hide tiers.

Where the data and the science come from

The foods and their nutrition facts come from open, established databases; the fullness score comes from published satiety research.

  • Food data – Open Food Facts, a free crowd-sourced database of millions of products worldwide. We keep the truly unprocessed whole foods (NOVA group 1) in every language, translating non-English names so the same food merges across languages (French tomates, German tomaten and English tomatoes become one point).
  • Nutrient reference – USDA FoodData Central.
  • How filling a food is – we score satiety per calorie with the Fullness Factor (NutritionData, US patent 7,620,531), the composition formula built and scaled against Holt et al.'s 1995 Satiety Index. A deep review of the evidence sets the weights: energy density is the single most robust driver (a ~30% lower energy density cuts intake ~30%), with fibre and protein adding modestly and fat subtracting; glycaemic index has no measurable effect. We apply two evidence-based corrections – potatoes (which the formula under-rates, yet topped Holt's measured index) and pulses (~31% more satiety per calorie, Li et al. 2014).
  • Processing level – the NOVA classification (Monteiro et al.), used to keep only whole, unprocessed foods.

Key sources: Holt 1995 Satiety Index, Fullness Factor (patent), Buckland 2015 satiety map, Rolls on energy density, plus Wikipedia, Optimising Nutrition and Nourished by Science. Full method: technical paper (PDF).

Why this is about as accurate as a model like this can be

The score is built from the factors satiety research has shown matter most – and nothing it can't justify:

  • It uses the strongest predictors, weighted by the evidence. A meta-analysis-level review finds energy density is the single most robust driver, with fibre and protein adding modestly and fat subtracting – exactly how this score weights them (glycaemic index, which has no measured effect, is left out).
  • It is checked against human measurements, not just against itself. Two independent human datasets, and it holds up against both. It reproduces the ordering of Holt's measured Satiety Index (rank correlation 0.75 over his 38 fed-and-measured foods) — slightly better than the published model built from 1,127 people's satiety ratings (Buckland et al. 2015), which scores 0.70 on the same benchmark, and well ahead of energy density alone (0.64). Run over 16,446 real recipes, this score and that independent human-derived model agree at 0.75. Adding more label variables does not reliably improve out-of-sample accuracy, so it sits near the practical ceiling for any score computed from a nutrition panel alone.
  • The ranking is robust. Scores are percentile ranks, so a few odd data points can't distort the scale and every food is directly comparable. Each food counts once, however many label variants it merged.
  • The data is cleaned. Foods are filtered to Open Food Facts' unprocessed group (NOVA 1) and every food's calories are reconciled against its protein, carbs and fat. That filter is the database's own label, not ours, and it lets through a handful of fermented and preserved foods – sauerkraut, kimchi, natto, tempeh, seaweed, cured ham – so "unprocessed" here means "NOVA 1", not literally raw.

In short, for a score you can compute from any nutrition label, this is about as accurate as it gets – the limitations below cover what no label-based score can do.

Limitations – what this can and can't tell you
  • Fullness is personal. It depends on you, the meal, how a food is prepared, and habit. These scores are a population-level estimate, not a promise about any one person or meal.
  • It's a relative ranking. A score of 80 means "more filling per calorie than about 80% of these foods", not an absolute amount of fullness.
  • It only sees the label. Working from calories, protein, fibre and fat, it can't fully capture texture, how solid or liquid a food is, blood-sugar response, or how foods combine in a meal.
  • Crowd-sourced data has errors. Open Food Facts is community-entered; we filter and sanity-check, but some mistakes will slip through.
  • It's a slice, not everything. This view is unprocessed whole foods (names translated to English) – not every food in the world.
  • Some foods beat their macros. Potatoes top measured tests by more than their calories, protein and fibre predict (structure and starch effects a label can't see); we correct potatoes and pulses from the measured data, but other such effects remain, so treat near-neighbours as roughly tied.

This is for general interest and ranking – not medical or dietary advice.
